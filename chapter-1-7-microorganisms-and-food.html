Section 1

Food Spoilage

Food spoilage is any undesirable change to a food — physical, chemical or microbiological. Most spoilage is microbiological, resulting in chemical changes that alter colour, texture and odour.

75°C
minimum cooking temperature to kill most pathogenic bacteria
🟡 Food Spoilage
Definition: Undesirable changes to food from physical, chemical or microbiological activity.

Causes: Fungi (moulds on bread, cheese, fruit) and non-pathogenic bacteria. Physical damage (bruising) increases surface area for microbial growth.

Detection: Usually detectable — changes to colour, texture and odour act as sensory warnings.

Risk: Spoilt food is less nutritious and can cause illness (especially fungal poisoning), but generally less dangerous than food poisoning.
🔴 Food Poisoning
Definition: Contamination by pathogenic (disease-causing) microorganisms that multiply exponentially and release toxins.

Symptoms: Fatigue, vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration.

Detection: Often undetectable — most food poisoning bacteria and their toxins have no smell or taste. Contamination commonly goes unnoticed.

⚠️ Key fact: Cooking above 75°C kills bacteria but does not destroy toxins already present in the food.
⚠️ Important Distinction — Toxins Survive Cooking
Heating food to 75°C or above kills most pathogenic bacteria, but the toxins they have already released remain in the food. This is why it is critical to prevent contamination before it occurs — not just to rely on cooking as a safeguard.
Section 2

Hygienic Practices

Safe food handling is required both in food businesses and at home. Applying proper hygiene can prevent most of the 5.4 million foodborne illness cases occurring each year in Australia.

🧼
Handwashing
Wash hands rigorously with soap for 20 seconds then rinse. Soap molecules bind to and destroy microbial cell membranes (lipid bilayer). Friction from rubbing removes and destroys more microorganisms.
👕
Protective Clothing
Wear clean aprons, tie back long hair and use hair nets. Cover all cuts and wounds with a band-aid or gloves to prevent transfer of microorganisms from wounds to food.
🤧
Personal Behaviour
Never cough or sneeze over food or preparation areas. Anyone experiencing diarrhoea or vomiting should not prepare food for others.
🔪
Clean Surfaces & Utensils
Properly clean and sanitise all surfaces and utensils involved in food preparation. Use separate cutting boards for meats and vegetables. Cooked food must never contact surfaces used for raw meat.
📱
High-Touch Surfaces
Wash hands frequently — especially after touching digital devices, changing nappies or handling raw meats. These surfaces carry many microorganisms that can transfer to food easily.
🛒
Purchasing Food
Check packaging for damage, swelling (indicates microbial gas production) or slits. Verify use-by dates. Pack cold foods in insulated bags. Keep hot and cold foods separate during transport.

How Soap Destroys Microorganisms

Soap molecules have a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and hydrophobic (water-avoiding) tail that targets lipid membranes.

1
🦠
Virus/Bacteria has Lipid Membrane
Microorganisms carry a membrane made of oily lipid molecules studded with proteins that help them infect cells.
2
💧
Soap Wedges into Membrane
The hydrophobic tails of soap molecules wedge into the lipid membrane and pry it apart — destroying the microorganism.
3
🫧
Micelles Wash Away
Soap traps dirt and destroyed virus/bacteria fragments in tiny bubbles called micelles, which rinse away with water.
Section 3

Food Preparation

Three critical preparation steps reduce microbial contamination: proper defrosting, eliminating cross contamination, and cooking to the correct temperature.

🧊
Defrosting
Always defrost food in a fridge, never on a bench. Room temperature puts food in the temperature danger zone (5–60°C), promoting microbial growth and division.
⚠️
Cross Contamination
Use separate cutting boards and utensils for meats and vegetables. Raw foods contain living microorganisms that can transfer to other foods, surfaces or equipment — known as cross contamination.
🌡️
Cooking Temperature
Cook or reheat food to 75°C or above — verified with a food thermometer. High-risk foods like raw chicken must be cooked all the way through (well done) with no uncooked sections.
🐔 High-Risk Foods — Raw Chicken
Unlike beef, chicken must be cooked well done — all the way through. Even a small uncooked section can harbour enough live pathogenic bacteria to cause food poisoning. Use a food thermometer to verify the internal temperature reaches 75°C or above.
Section 4

Food Storage

Proper food storage maximises shelf life and reduces food poisoning risk. The placement of food in a fridge is critical — different zones have different temperatures.

Recommended Fridge Organisation

Set fridge between 0°C and 5°C. Each shelf has a purpose — incorrect placement can lead to cross contamination.

🌡️ Fridge: Set between 0°C – 5°C
Top Shelf
Ready-to-eat foods: dairy products, yoghurts, creams, margarine, jams, sauces, and other packaged foods. These have no further cooking step to kill bacteria.
Middle Shelf
Cooked meats and covered leftovers. Store cooked food in shallow airtight containers — large pots can take up to 24 hours to cool to 5°C in the centre, keeping the interior in the danger zone.
Lower Shelf
Raw meat, poultry and fish — always in sealed containers. Stored below cooked foods to prevent juices leaking down and contaminating cooked items on lower shelves.
Salad Drawer
Fruit and vegetables in sealed bags or containers. The drawer regulates airflow and humidity. Do not wash vegetables before refrigerating — moisture promotes microbial growth. Wash immediately before use.

Key Storage Rules

⏰
Cooked food left in the danger zone for more than 4 hours should be discarded.
❄️
'Cool rapidly': to 21°C within 2 hours, then 5°C within a further 4 hours.
🥩
High-risk foods like meats and expressed breastmilk go at the back of the fridge — coldest zone, not the door.
🌾
Large bags of grains should not sit on the floor — this allows pest access (cockroaches, mice, pantry moths).
📦
Store dry foods in airtight containers to prevent pantry moth infestation and maintain shelf life.
🛍️
Check packaging for swollen cans (microbial gas), slits, or damage — these increase spoilage risk.

Food Preservation Methods

Food preservation aims to eliminate microorganisms already on food, deactivate enzymes causing spoilage, and prevent future contamination. It ensures a safe, continuous food supply and extends shelf life. Often multiple methods are combined.

🔥 Heating Techniques — kills microorganisms & deactivates enzymes
Cooking
KILLS BACTERIA
Cooking above 75°C eliminates microorganisms. Methods include frying, baking, boiling, steaming, blanching and microwaving. Does not destroy toxins already produced.
Canning Sterilisation
KILLS BACTERIA + SEALS
Food is placed in cans or jars and heated above 100°C. The process drives out oxygen and the container is vacuum sealed, preventing air exposure and re-contamination.
Pasteurisation
REDUCES BACTERIA
Standard treatment: 72°C for 15–20 seconds. Pasteurised products are not fully sterile — they still require refrigeration to maintain shelf life.
UHT Pasteurisation
STERILE + SHELF STABLE
Heating to 138°C for 2 seconds. When placed in an aseptic container, UHT products last 6–12 months at room temperature without refrigeration (e.g. long-life milk).
❄️ Reducing Temperature — slows microbial growth below the danger zone
Refrigeration
SLOWS GROWTH
Set between 0°C and 5°C (generally at 3°C). Keeps food out of the temperature danger zone. Bacteria survive but multiply very slowly. Food lasts days to weeks depending on the product.
Deep Freezing
DORMANT
Below −18°C, water in food converts to ice — reducing water activity to 0.0. Bacteria become dormant (no growth). Enzyme activity involved in spoilage is also significantly reduced.
💧 Reducing Water Activity (aw) — prevents bacterial reproduction (does not kill)
Dehydration / Smoking
REMOVES MOISTURE
Air drying, commercial dehydrators, or smoking remove moisture from food. Microbes lose water and become dormant. Methods include dried fruits, jerky, and smoked meats.
Salt & Sugar Additives
OSMOSIS
Salt (cured meats) and sugar (jams) bind to free water molecules, reducing water availability. The higher extracellular solute concentration draws water out of microbes by osmosis, further dehydrating them.
⚗️ Reducing pH (Acidification) — kills microorganisms by deactivating essential enzymes
Pickling with Acetic Acid
pH ≈ 2.4
Acetic acid (vinegar) has a pH of ~2.4. Food does not need to be sterile before immersion — the acidic environment kills most microorganisms. Most microbes cannot survive below pH 4.5.
Saltwater + Lactic Acid Bacteria
pH ≈ 3.5
Saltwater dehydrates food and promotes anaerobic bacteria to ferment, producing lactic acid (pH ~3.5) as a byproduct. This progressively lowers the pH until it becomes unsuitable for microbial survival.
Chemical Preservatives
LOWERS pH
Additives such as sodium benzoate, sodium nitrate and sulfur dioxide are weak acids that lower food pH, disrupting normal cellular metabolism of microorganisms.
💨 Oxygen Reduction — inhibits aerobic microorganisms & reduces oxidation (does not kill)
Vacuum Sealing
REMOVES O₂
Atmospheric gases are removed and the packaging is sealed tightly. Reduces oxygen available for aerobic microorganisms and slows oxidation of food (e.g. vacuum-packaged steak).
Oxygen Absorbers
ABSORBS O₂
Small chemical packets inserted into packaging (e.g. pita wraps) that absorb oxygen from within the sealed environment — reducing it for both aerobic microbes and oxidative reactions.
Modified Atmosphere Packaging (MAP)
REPLACES O₂
Atmospheric gas is removed and replaced with a specific gas mixture optimised for each product. Example: processed meats like salami use 30% CO₂ and 70% nitrogen to extend shelf life.
✨ Additional Techniques
Food Irradiation
IONISING RADIATION
Certain foods are exposed to gamma rays, X-rays or high-energy electron beams. The ionising radiation destroys insects, parasites, bacteria and other microorganisms involved in spoilage. Generally has less effect on appearance, flavour and texture than heat methods.
✓ Why Food Preservation Matters
Food preservation ensures a safe, continuous and plentiful food supply by reducing nutrient loss and extending shelf life. It makes some foods available year-round, saves preparation time and reduces costs. Without it, food options would be severely limited and shopping would be needed far more frequently.
