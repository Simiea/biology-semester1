Section 1

Structure of the Cell Membrane

The cell membrane is found in all living cells. It is called semi-permeable (or selectively permeable) because some molecules can pass through it but not all — based on size, charge and water solubility.

🔵
Phospholipid Bilayer
Two layers of phospholipid molecules. Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic (water-loving) phosphate head that faces the aqueous environments, and two hydrophobic (water-fearing) fatty acid tails that point inward — away from water.
🔩
Integral Proteins
Proteins fully integrated (embedded) into the phospholipid bilayer. Include transport proteins, channel proteins and carrier proteins. Span the entire width of the membrane.
📎
Peripheral Proteins
Proteins that attach to the internal or external surfaces of the bilayer — they do not span the full width. Include receptor proteins, adhesion proteins and some enzymes.
🍬
Glycoproteins & Glycolipids
Glycoproteins = carbohydrate chains attached to membrane proteins. Glycolipids = carbohydrate chains attached to phospholipids. Both act as cell identity markers — help the immune system distinguish self from non-self cells.
🧈
Cholesterol
A lipid embedded within the phospholipids. Contributes to the fluid nature of the membrane — maintaining its flexibility. Helps regulate membrane fluidity at varying temperatures.
🔑 The Phospholipid Arrangement
The bilayer structure arises naturally from the physical properties of phospholipids. Hydrophilic heads face outward toward the watery cytoplasm and extracellular fluid. Hydrophobic tails project inward toward each other — creating a water-excluding interior that forms a barrier to most polar and charged molecules.
Section 2

Membrane Proteins & Functions

The cell membrane performs many functions beyond being a simple barrier. A variety of proteins embedded in the bilayer are responsible for most of these functions.

Protein Type Function Example
Transport Proteins
(channels & carriers)		 Control the selective exchange of molecules between the cell and its external environment. Specific to particular molecules. Aquaporins (water channels), GLUT4 (glucose carrier), Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump
Receptor Proteins Allow communication — bind complementary hormones or signalling molecules to trigger a cellular response. Insulin receptors on muscle cells; hormone receptors
Recognition Proteins
(glycoproteins)		 Act as cell identity markers — allow the immune system to identify the organism's own ("self") cells vs foreign ("non-self") cells. MHC proteins on human cells; ABO blood group antigens
Adhesion Proteins Stick out and bind to adjacent cells, providing structural support and helping to maintain cell shape. Integrins; cadherins in tissue junctions
Enzymes Catalyse specific biochemical reactions at the cell surface. ATPase enzymes on mitochondrial membranes
Section 3

The Fluid Mosaic Model

Proposed by S.J. Singer and G.L. Nicholson in 1972, the fluid mosaic model is the accepted scientific model for visualising cell membrane structure.

"Fluid"
Refers to the ability of both lipids and proteins to continually move laterally within the membrane. This fluidity is critical for membrane permeability — the ease with which molecules can cross the membrane. Cholesterol helps regulate this fluidity.
"Mosaic"
Refers to the pattern formed by the variety of proteins embedded within and on the surface of the phospholipid bilayer — resembling a mosaic tile pattern. These proteins are fundamental to the function of the membrane.
Section 4

Selective Permeability

Different molecules cross the cell membrane at very different rates — based on their size, water solubility (polarity) and charge. This is what makes the membrane "semi-permeable" or "selectively permeable."

High Permeability
CO₂ O₂ ethanol Small, non-polar, lipid-soluble gases and molecules — pass freely through the bilayer
Moderate Permeability
water (H₂O) urea Very small uncharged polar molecules — can slip through slowly
Low Permeability
glucose amino acids polar organic molecules Larger polar molecules — need transport proteins
Very Low Permeability
Na⁺ / K⁺ ions ATP proteins nucleic acids polysaccharides Charged, large, or macromolecules — cannot pass without assistance
💡 Two Key Properties Control Selectivity
(1) The lipid nature of the bilayer — hydrophobic interior blocks water-soluble (hydrophilic) and charged molecules.
(2) The specificity of transport proteins — each type of channel or carrier protein is selective for particular molecules, giving the membrane fine-grained control over what enters and exits.
Section 5

Passive Transport

Passive transport is movement that does not require energy (ATP). Molecules move down their concentration gradient — from high to low concentration — driven by the random kinetic motion of atoms and molecules.

💨 Simple Diffusion
Movement of molecules from high concentration to low concentration directly through the phospholipid bilayer — no transport protein needed.

Steeper gradient = greater rate of diffusion.

Molecules: Small, non-polar molecules — O₂, CO₂, ethanol, lipid-soluble molecules.
No energy needed Down gradient No protein needed
🚪 Facilitated Diffusion
Movement down the concentration gradient via specific transport proteins (channels or carriers) embedded in the bilayer. Still passive — no energy needed.

Molecules: Hydrophilic molecules (glucose, amino acids) and ions (Na⁺, K⁺) that cannot cross the lipid bilayer directly.
No energy needed Down gradient Transport protein required
💧 Osmosis
The diffusion of water from an area of high water concentration (low solute) to an area of low water concentration (high solute) through a semi-permeable membrane.

Water can pass directly through the bilayer or through aquaporin channel proteins. Solute molecules bind to water, reducing free water molecules.
No energy needed Water only Aquaporins help
Section 6

Osmosis: Hypotonic, Isotonic & Hypertonic

The effects of osmosis on a cell depend on the relative solute concentration of the solution outside vs inside the cell. The typical solute concentration of animal and plant cells is approximately 0.9%.

💦
Hypotonic
Low solute · High water · <0.9%
🐾 Animal cell: Water moves IN → cell swells → may burst (lysis)
🌿 Plant cell: Water moves IN → cell swells → becomes turgid (firm). Cell wall prevents bursting.
⚖️
Isotonic
Equal solute · Equal water · ~0.9%
🐾 Animal cell: No net movement of water → normal shape
🌿 Plant cell: No net movement → normal. Dynamic equilibrium — water moves equally in both directions.
🏜️
Hypertonic
High solute · Low water · >0.9%
🐾 Animal cell: Water moves OUT → cell shrivels → may die
🌿 Plant cell: Water moves OUT → cell membrane pulls away from wall → plasmolysis
⚠️ Why Plant Cells Handle Osmosis Better
Plant cells have a cell wall that prevents them from bursting in hypotonic environments — the wall limits further water entry once the cell is turgid. Animal cells lack this wall, making them more vulnerable to cell lysis in hypotonic environments and crenation (shrivelling) in hypertonic ones.
Section 7

Active Transport

Active transport moves molecules against the concentration gradient — from low to high concentration. This requires an input of energy (ATP). Used when passive transport cannot meet the cell's needs.

⚡ Active Transport
Carrier proteins use ATP energy to pump solutes against their concentration gradient (low → high concentration).

Example: Absorption of glucose from the small intestine when glucose concentration in the intestine is lower than in the epithelial cells.
ATP required Against gradient Carrier protein
📥 Endocytosis
Large molecules or bulk particles outside the cell are engulfed. The cell membrane folds inward around the material and pinches off to form a vesicle inside the cell.

Example: White blood cells engulfing bacteria (phagocytosis).
ATP required Bulk INTO cell Vesicle formed
📤 Exocytosis
Large molecules or bulk materials packaged in vesicles inside the cell (e.g. by the Golgi body) are transported to the membrane. The vesicle fuses with the cell membrane and releases contents to the extracellular environment.

Example: Secretion of digestive enzymes, hormones, or neurotransmitters.
ATP required Bulk OUT of cell Vesicle fuses
Section 8

Active vs Passive Transport — Comparison

Feature ✅ Passive Transport ⚡ Active Transport
Concentration Gradient With gradient — high → low concentration Against gradient — low → high concentration
Energy (ATP) Required? No — no energy needed Yes — ATP required
Transport Protein Needed? Not always — simple diffusion needs none; facilitated diffusion and osmosis use proteins Yes — carrier proteins (and membrane movement for endo/exocytosis)
Examples Simple diffusion (O₂, CO₂), facilitated diffusion (glucose, ions), osmosis (water) Active transport (Na⁺/K⁺ pump), endocytosis (engulfing bacteria), exocytosis (secretion of hormones)
Molecules Moved Small non-polar molecules, water, hydrophilic molecules & ions (with proteins) Any molecules the cell requires, including large/bulk materials via endo/exocytosis
Section 9

Surface Area to Volume Ratio (SA:V)

The SA:V ratio represents the relationship between a cell's external surface area (cell membrane available for exchange) and its internal volume (cytoplasm that needs to be supplied). It is critical to cell survival.

r = 1
Small Cell
SA:V = 3:1
Large ratio — very efficient exchange. Materials can reach all parts of the cytoplasm easily.
r = 2
Medium Cell
SA:V = 1.5:1
Ratio decreasing. Exchange efficiency drops as volume grows faster than surface area.
r = 3
Large Cell
SA:V = 1:1
Small ratio — inefficient exchange. Cannot sustain life — too little membrane relative to volume.
📐 Why Cells Stay Microscopic
As a cell grows, volume increases faster than surface area. When the SA:V ratio drops too low, the cell membrane cannot exchange materials fast enough to supply the cytoplasm — the cell cannot survive. This is why cells divide when they grow too large rather than continuing to grow. A decreasing SA:V ratio is a key stimulus for cell division.

Factors Affecting Rate of Exchange Across the Membrane

Factor How it Affects Exchange Rate
Size Only very small molecules (e.g. O₂, CO₂) pass freely. Larger molecules require transport proteins or endo/exocytosis.
Water Solubility Hydrophilic molecules cannot cross the lipid bilayer easily — they need specific transport proteins. Lipophilic (fat-soluble) molecules like steroid hormones pass through easily.
Charge Ions are charged and highly water-soluble — they cannot cross the bilayer without specific ion channel or carrier proteins.
Concentration Gradient Greater difference in concentration between two regions = greater rate of diffusion. Applies to all passive transport. In osmosis, it is the solute concentration gradient that drives water movement.
SA:V Ratio Higher SA:V ratio = more membrane surface available relative to volume = more efficient exchange. Small cells have high SA:V ratios — critical for survival.
