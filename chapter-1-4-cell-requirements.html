Section 1

Cells Require Energy

All cells constantly perform processes — movement, synthesis of materials, and maintaining a stable internal environment. All of these require a continuous input of energy. The primary energy source for life on Earth is the sun.

Process Type Examples in Cells and Organisms
Movement Contraction of muscle cells · Spinning of sperm flagella · Movement of chromosomes by spindle fibres during cell division
Synthesis of Materials DNA replication · Protein synthesis at ribosomes · Glucose synthesis through photosynthesis
Maintaining Stability Regulating ion and waste concentrations in cells · Maintaining constant blood temperature · Regulating pH and blood glucose levels

ATP — The Energy Currency of the Cell

Glucose stores chemical energy, but cells cannot use it directly. Respiration converts glucose into ATP (adenosine triphosphate) — the cell's immediate, usable form of energy.

ATP Structure
Adenine — Ribose — P — P — P
ATP has a triphosphate tail (three phosphate groups). The bond between the 2nd and 3rd phosphate stores energy. Breaking this bond releases usable energy for the cell to do work.
ATP ⇌ ADP Cycle
ATP → ADP + Pᵢ + Energy
When ATP is broken down to ADP (adenosine diphosphate) + a free phosphate group (Pᵢ), energy is released. ADP can be recharged back to ATP using energy from respiration — a continuous cycle.
Section 2

Autotrophs & Heterotrophs

All organisms need energy-rich organic molecules (e.g. glucose). How they obtain them defines whether they are an autotroph or a heterotroph.

🌿 Autotrophs (Producers)
Can synthesise their own organic energy-rich molecules from simple inorganic molecules (CO₂ and H₂O).

Two types:
1. Photosynthetic — use light energy (plants, algae, cyanobacteria)
2. Chemosynthetic — use chemical energy (sulfur bacteria at hydrothermal vents)

Ecosystems depend on autotrophs to produce the energy-rich molecules that fuel all life.
🦁 Heterotrophs (Consumers)
Cannot synthesise their own organic molecules — must obtain them by consuming other organisms or parts of them.

Rely entirely on autotrophs (directly or indirectly) for their supply of energy-rich molecules.

Examples: all animals, fungi, most bacteria. They eat plants directly, or eat animals that have eaten plants.

Comparing Photo- vs Chemo-Autotrophs

Type Energy Source Chlorophyll? By-Products Examples
Photosynthetic Light (from sun) Yes Oxygen Plants, algae, cyanobacteria
Chemosynthetic Chemical (e.g. H₂S, CH₄) No Depends on reaction type Sulfur bacteria (deep-ocean hydrothermal vents), nitrogen-fixing bacteria
🌋 Chemosynthesis — Life Without Sunlight
In deep-ocean environments where light cannot penetrate, chemosynthetic organisms like sulfur bacteria use inorganic molecules (e.g. hydrogen sulfide, H₂S) released from hydrothermal vents as their energy source. They form the base of entire ecosystems that exist without sunlight.
Section 3

Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis occurs in photosynthetic autotrophs. Light energy is trapped by the green pigment chlorophyll and used to convert inorganic molecules (CO₂ and H₂O) into the organic molecule glucose, releasing oxygen as a by-product.

🌿 Photosynthesis Equations
Word: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
(requires: light energy + chlorophyll)

Limiting Factors of Photosynthesis

Factors that prevent photosynthesis reaching its maximum rate are called limiting factors. Understanding them helps humans maximise crop yields.

☀️ Light Intensity
Increasing light intensity increases the rate of photosynthesis in a linear trend — until another factor (e.g. CO₂ concentration) becomes limiting, at which point the rate plateaus.
💨 CO₂ Concentration
If CO₂ concentration is increased when light intensity has become limiting, the rate of photosynthesis will rise again — until yet another factor limits it.
🔵 Light Wavelength / Colour
Chlorophyll absorbs violet/blue and red wavelengths most readily — peaks of photosynthesis occur at these wavelengths. Green light is poorly absorbed, so photosynthesis rate drops in green light.
🌾 Why It Matters
Humans depend on photosynthetic organisms for food (fruits, vegetables, livestock feed). Manipulating limiting factors can increase crop yields, making this knowledge economically vital.
Section 4

Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the metabolic pathway that converts glucose into ATP — the usable energy currency for cells. There are two types: aerobic (with oxygen) and anaerobic (without oxygen).

Aerobic Respiration

Occurs in the presence of sufficient oxygen. Involves mitochondria. Produces 36 ATP — a large yield due to the complete breakdown of glucose.

⚡ Aerobic Respiration Equations
Word: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ ATP energy)
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O (+36 ATP)

Section 5

Anaerobic Respiration (Fermentation)

When oxygen is insufficient — such as during intense exercise — cells switch to anaerobic respiration (fermentation). It occurs only in the cytoplasm, produces only 2 ATP, and results in an incomplete breakdown of glucose.

🍷 Ethanol (Alcohol) Fermentation
Occurs in: Plants and yeast

Word equation:
glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide

Chemical equation:
C₆H₁₂O₆ → C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

Used industrially to produce bread (CO₂ makes dough rise), wine and beer (ethanol).
💪 Lactic Acid Fermentation
Occurs in: Animals and some bacteria

Word equation:
glucose → lactic acid

Chemical equation:
C₆H₁₂O₆ → C₃H₆O₃

Occurs in human muscle cells during vigorous exercise when O₂ delivery cannot meet demand.

Section 6

Material Requirements

As well as energy, cells need water, macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids), micronutrients (vitamins and minerals), and nucleic acids. Different organisms obtain these in different ways.

🌿
Autotrophs — Sources
Photosynthetic autotrophs source CO₂ from air (through stomata), water from soil (via roots/xylem), and light from the sun. They also absorb at least 14 mineral nutrients dissolved in water from soil.
🍎
Heterotrophs — Sources
Must consume other organisms. Carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids are broken down in the digestive system into building blocks (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, nucleotides) and absorbed through the small intestine.
💧
Water
Essential for all chemical reactions in cells. Autotrophs absorb it from soil or water bodies. Heterotrophs obtain it from food and by drinking. Water molecules can be bound to salts or structures, making them unavailable.
🧬
Macronutrients
Carbohydrates (energy), proteins (structure and enzymes), lipids (membranes and energy storage). Heterotrophs digest these into their building blocks for absorption. Autotrophs synthesise their own via photosynthesis.
⚗️
Micronutrients
Vitamins and minerals required in small amounts for specific roles (e.g. enzyme function, cell membrane structure). Plants need at least 14 mineral nutrients — notably nitrogen and phosphorus.
🔬
Nucleic Acids
DNA and RNA are required for storing and expressing genetic information. Some organisms can synthesise their own nucleic acids; others may need to consume them. Nucleotides are absorbed after digestion.

Why Nitrogen & Phosphorus Are Vital

These two minerals are required in large amounts by plants and are incorporated into some of the most fundamental molecules of life.

Section 7

Waste Production & Removal

Some metabolic reactions produce waste products that are toxic if they build up. These must be excreted from cells and eventually removed from the organism to maintain a suitable internal environment for chemical reactions.

💨 Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)
Produced by: All cells in aerobic respiration; plant and yeast cells in ethanol fermentation.

In animals: Excreted into blood → transported to lungs → diffuses into lungs → exhaled.

In plants: Transported to leaf cells → used as a reactant in photosynthesis.
🟡 Urea
Produced by: Breakdown (metabolism) of proteins in liver cells of animals.

Removal: Excreted into blood → kidneys filter urea → excreted as urine via urination.

In plants: Urea contains nitrogen — plants can absorb urea from soil and use it for essential molecules. Some need bacteria to convert it first.
💪 Lactic Acid
Produced by: Lactic acid fermentation in animal cells and some bacteria.

Toxic if accumulated: Denatures proteins and can cause cell death. Contributes to muscle fatigue and soreness during strenuous exercise.

Removal: Excreted into blood → transported to liver → metabolised with oxygen, releasing additional energy.
🍷 Ethanol
Produced by: Ethanol fermentation in plant and yeast cells.

Toxic if accumulated: Can denature proteins and cause cell death at high concentrations.

Further use: Ethanol is involved in further metabolic pathways in plant and yeast cells, making some energy available for cellular use.
⚠️ Why Waste Removal Is Essential
Waste products such as CO₂, urea, lactic acid and ethanol are toxic at high concentrations — they can denature proteins, disrupting enzyme function and eventually causing cell death. Continuous excretion is necessary to maintain a stable internal cellular environment (homeostasis) where chemical reactions can occur efficiently.
