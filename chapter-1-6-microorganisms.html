Overview

What are Microorganisms?

Microorganisms (microbes) are living organisms too small to be seen with the naked eye. They are a diverse group — both unicellular and multicellular — making up approximately 60% of all living matter on Earth. They exist in virtually every environment, performing crucial roles from nutrient recycling to oxygen production.

🦠
Bacteria
Unicellular prokaryotes with peptidoglycan cell walls. Reproduce by binary fission. Found in every environment — helpful and harmful.
🌿
Archaea
Prokaryotes with unique lipid cell walls. Extremophiles — methanogens, halophiles, thermophiles and psychrophiles. Their own domain of life.
🍄
Fungi
Eukaryotes with chitin cell walls. Unicellular (yeast) or multicellular (moulds, mushrooms). Heterotrophic — absorb nutrients from environment.
🔬
Protists
Largest group of organisms on Earth — 100,000+ species. Includes protozoa, algae (phytoplankton) and slime moulds. Highly diverse.
⚡
Viruses
Non-cellular entities with DNA or RNA, proteins and lipids. Not considered living as they cannot carry out life's processes independently.
🌍
Their Scale
An estimated 100 trillion microorganisms live on or within the human body — roughly equal to the number of human cells.

Microbiology

Conditions for Bacterial Growth

Under optimal conditions, a single bacterium divides every ~20 minutes by binary fission, reaching ~2 million in 7 hours and 68 billion in 12 hours. Six key factors govern whether this exponential growth occurs.

Factor Detail Key Example
🧪 Nutrients Carbon, nitrogen, sulphur and phosphorus required. Autotrophic bacteria use inorganic sources; heterotrophic bacteria need organic molecules. Saprophytes use decaying matter. Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron — 460+ carbohydrate-digesting enzymes
💨 Oxygen Aerobes require O₂ to respire. Anaerobes do not need O₂. Facultative anaerobes can function with or without O₂. E. coli (aerobe) · C. botulinum (anaerobe — survives in tinned food)
💧 Moisture Water activity (aw) scale 0–1.0. Most pathogens require aw > 0.9. Below 0.75 inhibits bacterial growth. Below 0.6 prevents all microbial growth. Honey (aw 0.5–0.7) — too low for most bacteria
🌡️ Temperature Danger zone: 5°C–60°C. Psychrophiles thrive in cold; thermophiles in heat. Most bacteria die above 75°C. At −18°C bacteria become dormant. Salmonella dies at 71°C
⚗️ pH Most bacteria prefer pH 6.6–7.5 (neutral). Some prefer acidic or alkaline environments. pH outside optimal range inhibits enzyme function. Stomach acid (very low pH) limits bacterial colonisation
🗑️ Waste Removal Metabolic waste products are toxic to bacteria. If wastes accumulate they inhibit growth. Efficient removal is essential for continued exponential growth. Lactic acid bacteria lower gut pH — inhibiting pathogens

Human Health

The Human Microbiome

The human microbiome is the collection of all microorganisms — bacteria, fungi, protozoans and viruses — that inhabit the human body. There are an estimated 100 trillion microorganisms on or within us, roughly equal to the number of human cells. Only 10–20% of an individual's bacteria are shared with others — each person's microbiome is unique.

Microbiome Diversity and Health

Like a biodiverse ecosystem, a more diverse microbiome is a more resilient one — able to recover from infections and antibiotics more easily.

✓ High Diversity (Favourable)

  • More resilient to disturbances
  • Recovers faster after antibiotics
  • High Faecalibacterium (protective)
  • High Bacteroidales
  • Associated with better health outcomes

✗ Low Diversity (Unfavourable)

  • Less resilient and harder to restore
  • Associated with antibiotic overuse
  • Higher A. muciniphila and E. hirae
  • Increased disease risk
  • Like a destabilised ecosystem
Digestive System

Roles of the Gut Microbiome

The gut microbiome (weighing ~1.5 kg, with up to 1,000 species) lives mostly in the large intestine. Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes make up two thirds of gut microbes. It plays vital roles in digestion, immunity and overall health.

⚡ Energy Extraction
Bacteria digest carbohydrates we cannot — especially fibre and resistant starch. Up to 30% of our daily energy is produced by gut bacteria. B. thetaiotaomicron alone has 460+ carbohydrate-digesting enzymes (360 more than humans).
🧪 Vitamin Production
Bacteria release vitamins from food, increasing their bioavailability. Vitamin K is a key example — infants with underdeveloped microbiomes cannot synthesise it efficiently and require a vitamin K injection at birth.
🛡️ Short Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs)
Fermenting dietary fibre produces SCFAs (acetate, propionate, butyrate). Butyrate nourishes the intestinal lining, reduces inflammation, and protects against intestinal cancer. SCFAs also have anti-obesity and anti-diabetic roles.
🦾 Immune System Support
Gut microorganisms are an essential component of a robust, balanced immune system. Some strains produce enzymes capable of destroying bacterial toxins. They also produce substances that inhibit or destroy potentially dangerous bacteria.
⚗️ pH Regulation
Some bacteria produce lactic acid as a byproduct of fermenting carbohydrates. This lowers the gut pH, making the environment less suitable for pathogenic microorganisms to grow — a natural defence mechanism.
Ecology & Environment

Wider Importance to Humans & Earth

🌊
Oxygen Production
Phytoplankton (microalgae/plant-like protists) perform photosynthesis in the ocean, producing an estimated 50% of Earth's oxygen. Prochlorococcus, the smallest phytoplankton, alone produces up to 20% — more than all tropical rainforests combined.
♻️
Nutrient Recycling
Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead organic matter into inorganic substances, returning them to soil and oceans. Without them, nutrients would be locked away and dead matter would accumulate. Essential for carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles.
🌾
Food Web Foundations
Phytoplankton are the primary producers of oceanic food webs, converting CO₂ to organic matter via photosynthesis. They are fed upon by zooplankton, small fish and whales. Decomposers return nutrients when they die, closing the cycle.
💊
Medicine & Biotechnology
Bacteria and fungi are used to produce insulin, antibiotics (penicillin), anti-clotting factors for haemophilia, human growth hormone (HGH) and vaccine antigens — revolutionising medicine through protein engineering.
🍞
Food Production
Humans have cultured microorganisms for ~10,000 years. Yeast ferments sugars to produce bread, wine and beer. Lactic acid bacteria create yoghurt, cheese and soy sauce. Fungi produce blue cheese and penicillin.
🧬
Genetic Engineering
Bacteria's short generation time, simple DNA and small size make them ideal for recombinant DNA technology. Scientists insert genes of interest into bacterial plasmids to produce useful proteins or create transgenic organisms with novel traits.
Biotechnology

Recombinant DNA Technology

Recombinant DNA technology (genetic engineering) produces artificial DNA by combining DNA from two or more sources. Microorganisms — especially bacteria — are ideal because of their short generation time, simple DNA, small size, and minimal ethical concerns.

Steps in Gene Cloning

01
Extract DNA and Plasmids
Target DNA (containing the gene of interest, e.g. insulin gene) and bacterial plasmids are extracted from their respective cells.
02
Cut with Restriction Enzymes
A specific restriction enzyme cuts both the target DNA and the bacterial plasmid at precise locations, creating "sticky ends" that are complementary.
03
Recombine with DNA Ligase
DNA ligase joins the target gene into the cut plasmid, forming recombinant DNA — a new plasmid containing the gene of interest.
04
Transformation into Bacteria
The recombinant plasmid is inserted back into bacteria through a process called transformation. The bacteria now carry the gene of interest.
05
Culture and Clone
Bacteria are cultured in optimal conditions. They divide by binary fission exponentially, each division replicating the plasmid — cloning the gene of interest.
06
Harvest or Transfer
For protein engineering: ribosomes synthesise the desired protein (e.g. insulin), which is extracted and purified. For transgenesis: genes are isolated and transferred into another organism's genome.

Examples of Transgenic Organisms

Organism Purpose
🍚 Golden Rice / Golden Bananas Increased vitamin A content to reduce vitamin A deficiency (VAD) disorders in Asia and Africa
🌱 Herbicide Resistant Crops Can be sprayed with herbicides — only weeds are killed, increasing yield through less competition
🐛 Pest Resistant Crops Secrete their own insecticides; increased yield and reduced chemical pollution
🦠 Disease Resistant Crops Immunity to specific diseases, e.g. GM potatoes resistant to PVY virus
🌿 Nitrogen Fixing Crops Synthesise nitrates from atmospheric N₂; reduces fertiliser use and eutrophication risk
♻️ Plastic Eating Bacteria Break down multiple plastic types rapidly — applicable to oceans and landfill
🐟 Fast Growing Atlantic Salmon Reaches full size in 2 years instead of 4 — reduces pressure on wild salmon populations
Applications

Human Uses of Microorganisms

Humans have cultured microorganisms such as bacteria and yeast for about 10,000 years to make food, beverages and medicines.

🍞
Bread
Saccharomyces cerevisiae (yeast)
Yeast ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂ gas that causes the dough to rise. The ethanol produced evaporates during baking.
🍷
Wine & Beer
Saccharomyces (yeast)
Yeast converts glucose in grape juice (or grain) into ethanol and CO₂ through alcoholic fermentation — producing wine, beer and spirits.
🥛
Yoghurt
Lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus)
Lactic acid bacteria ferment lactose in milk, producing lactic acid. This lowers pH, causes milk to clot, and creates yoghurt's characteristic texture and sour flavour.
🧀
Swiss Cheese
Lactobacilli + Propionibacterium
Lactic acid fermentation is followed by Propionic bacteria fermenting lactic acid to produce propionic acid, acetic acid and CO₂. The CO₂ forms the characteristic holes.
🧀
Blue Cheese
Lactobacilli + Penicillium roqueforti
Lactic acid bacteria ferment sugars. The mould Penicillium roqueforti is also added, producing the characteristic blue veins and distinctive sharp flavour.
💊
Penicillin
Penicillium chrysogenum (fungus)
Fungal cells are cultured in a fermenter with oxygen and lactose. Cells are subjected to stress to trigger penicillin production. The antibiotic is extracted and purified.
