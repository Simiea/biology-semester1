Section 1

The Purpose of Cell Division

New cells are formed by cell division — a process fundamental to the continuity of life. Only binary fission (prokaryotes) and mitotic division (eukaryotes) are covered in Chapter 1.3. Meiotic division for sexual reproduction is a Stage 2 Biology topic.

🧬
Transmit genetic information — DNA encoded in chromosomes is passed from generation to generation of cells.
🌱
Growth and development — multicellular organisms grow from a single fertilised ovum through repeated cell divisions.
🩹
Replace and repair cells — new cells replace dead cells or repair damaged tissue (e.g. intestinal lining replaced every 2–5 days).
🔄
Asexual reproduction — allows some organisms to produce new, genetically identical individuals without sexual reproduction.

Eukaryotes that reproduce asexually by mitotic division:

🦠 Unicellular amoeba
🪸 Multicellular hydra
⭐ Multicellular sea stars
🍓 Multicellular strawberry plants
Section 2

Transferring DNA Between Generations

Before a cell divides, its DNA must be replicated so each daughter cell receives an identical copy. Three essential events must occur in both binary fission and mitotic division.

1
DNA Replication: The cell's DNA is doubled before division begins. Each chromosome produces two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere.
2
Separation of DNA copies: The two copies of DNA are moved to opposite ends of the parent cell — ensuring each daughter cell receives exactly one copy.
3
Division of cytoplasm: The cytoplasm of the parent cell is divided (cytokinesis), forming two new daughter cells — each with a complete, identical set of genetic material.
🧬 Why DNA Replication Must Come First
If DNA replication does not occur before cell division, the parent cell has only one copy of its DNA — it cannot supply an identical copy to both daughter cells. Without replication, division would produce one cell with DNA and one without, breaking the continuity of genetic information.

Key Terms

Chromosome
A structure made of DNA and proteins. In eukaryotes, chromosomes are linear; in prokaryotes, the chromosome is circular.
Sister Chromatids
Two identical copies of a chromosome produced by DNA replication, joined together at the centromere.
Centromere
The point where sister chromatids are joined together after DNA replication.
Chromatin
The decondensed (uncoiled) form of DNA in eukaryotic cells. Present during interphase — not visible under a light microscope.
Parent Cell
The dividing cell that gives rise to two daughter cells. Must have its DNA replicated before dividing.
Daughter Cells
The two new cells produced by cell division. Are genetically identical clones of the parent cell.
Section 3

Binary Fission

Most unicellular prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) reproduce by binary fission. Because prokaryotic DNA is located in the cytoplasm rather than a nucleus, the process is relatively simple compared to mitotic division.

Steps of Binary Fission

1
The cell's circular chromosome attaches to the inside of the cell membrane.
2
DNA replication occurs, resulting in two identical circular chromosomes both attached to the cell membrane.
3
The cell elongates lengthways, which begins to physically separate the two chromosomes.
4
As elongation continues, the separated chromosomes are moved to opposite ends of the parent cell.
5
A new cell wall and cell membrane (cross wall) form through the centre of the elongated cell.
6
The cell divides into two daughter cells, each containing one identical circular chromosome.
🔬 Before Binary Fission Begins
Prior to binary fission, the cell's entire contents doubles — including the synthesis of more ribosomes, more plasmids, and the doubling of DNA. This preparation ensures each daughter cell is a fully functional, independent cell.
Section 4

Mitotic Division

Eukaryotic cells store DNA in a nucleus, requiring a more complex division process. Mitotic division involves two separate processes: Mitosis (division of the nucleus) followed by Cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).

🔵
Prophase
Chromosomes condense; nuclear membrane breaks down
⚖️
Metaphase
Chromosomes align at the equator (metaphase plate)
↔️
Anaphase
Sister chromatids pulled to opposite poles
🔮
Telophase
Nuclear membranes re-form; chromosomes decondense
✂️
Cytokinesis
Cytoplasm divides into two daughter cells
📋 Before Mitosis Begins — Preparation
DNA is initially in a decondensed chromatin form, making it easy for the replication machinery to access it. After DNA replication, the chromatin must condense (coil) tightly around histone proteins to form chromosomes. This protects the long DNA molecules and makes individual chromosomes visible under a light microscope. A human cell has 46 duplicated chromosomes — approximately 4 metres of DNA — condensed into manageable structures.
Section 5

The Four Phases of Mitosis

Mitosis is a continuous process during which the nucleus of the parent cell divides to produce two identical daughter nuclei. Four recognisable phases mark changes to chromosome visibility and location.

Phase 1
Prophase
  • Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes — each consisting of two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere
  • Nuclear membrane breaks down into small vesicles (not visible under light microscope)
  • Centrioles duplicate and move to opposite ends of the cell to form the poles of the spindle apparatus
  • Spindle fibres begin to radiate from the centrioles
Phase 2
Metaphase
  • Each centriole reaches a pole; spindle fibres attach to the centromeres of chromosomes now in the cytoplasm
  • Chromosomes are lined up along the equator of the cell (the metaphase plate) by the spindle apparatus
  • The spindle is made from protein microtubules
Phase 3
Anaphase
  • Each chromosome splits at the centromere — separating the sister chromatids
  • Spindle fibres pull sister chromatids to opposite poles, centromeres first
  • Results in identical sets of DNA at each pole of the cell
Phase 4
Telophase
  • Chromosomes at each pole are enclosed in a new nuclear membrane
  • Chromosomes decondense back into chromatin — they are no longer visible, signalling the end of mitosis
  • Each centriole at each pole ensures daughter cells have the cellular machinery for future nuclear division
  • Cytokinesis (division of cytoplasm) begins in telophase
Section 6

Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm (not the nucleus) and is considered separate from mitosis. It begins during anaphase or telophase and finishes after telophase.

1
Microfilaments ring forms: A ring of protein fibres (microfilaments) assembles around the equator of the cell, just inside the cell membrane.
2
Cleavage furrow forms: The microfilament ring constricts inward, creating an infolding of the cell membrane called a cleavage furrow.
3
Pinching complete: Constriction continues until the cytoplasm is fully divided — producing two separate, genetically identical daughter cells.
Section 7

Cancer & Uncontrolled Cell Division

Mitotic division is highly regulated in multicellular organisms. Loss of this regulation can have serious consequences.

⚠️ When Regulation Fails — Tumours and Cancer
If one cell loses control over the mechanisms regulating cell division, it can undergo uncontrolled cell division — forming a mass of cells known as a tumour or cancer. Factors that increase this risk include:

🔴 Exposure to ionising radiation  ·  🚬 Cigarette smoke  ·  🏗️ Asbestos  ·  🦠 Some viruses

The cell cycle that controls cell division is covered in detail in Stage 2 Biology.
Section 8

Binary Fission vs Mitotic Division

Both processes produce two genetically identical daughter cells, but there are important differences in how they achieve this.

Feature 🦠 Binary Fission 🔬 Mitotic Division
Type of Cell Prokaryotic Eukaryotic
DNA Replication Before Division? Yes — identical in both Yes — identical in both
Nuclear Division? No — prokaryotes have no nucleus Yes — nucleus must divide first
Type of Chromosome Single, circular chromosome Many linear chromosomes
Spindle Fibres Used? No Yes — to move chromosomes
Number of Cells Produced Two — identical in both Two — identical in both
Daughter Cells Clones of parent cell — identical in both Clones of parent cell — identical in both
Growth Pattern (no restrictions) Exponential — identical in both Exponential — identical in both
Complexity Relatively simple More complex — 4 phases
📌 Key Similarity
Despite their differences, both binary fission and mitotic division share the same three essential events: (1) DNA replication, (2) separation of DNA copies to opposite ends, and (3) division of the cytoplasm. The result in both cases is two genetically identical daughter cells — clones of the parent.
