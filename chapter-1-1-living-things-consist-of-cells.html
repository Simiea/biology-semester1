Section 1

The Cell Theory

The invention of the microscope enabled the discovery of cells. Robert Hooke named them "cells" in 1665 because cork cells reminded him of monastery rooms. Theodore Schwann proposed the original cell theory in the 19th century.

1665
Robert Hooke
Named microscopic structures "cells" — cork cells viewed under the microscope reminded him of the rooms (cells) in monasteries.
19th Century
Theodore Schwann
Proposed the original cell theory — a set of principles explaining how all living things are made up of cells.
Modern Day
Scientific Advancements
Three modern additions to the original theory, reflecting greater understanding of DNA, species similarity and energy flow within cells.
📜 Original Cell Theory
  • All organisms are made of cells
  • Cells are the basic units of life
  • Cells come from pre-existing cells that have multiplied
🔬 Three Modern Additions
  • DNA is passed between cells during cell division
  • Cells of all organisms within a similar species are mostly the same structurally and chemically
  • Energy flow occurs within cells
🔑 The Cell is the Smallest Independent Unit of Life
The cell is the smallest unit capable of independently carrying out all of life's fundamental processes. Organelles within cells cannot carry out these processes independently — they are therefore considered non-living.
Section 2

What Makes Something Alive?

The Earth is the only known place to sustain life. To biologists, something is alive if it carries out fundamental life processes. All living things carry out these same processes — this is what distinguishes them from non-living things.

🌍 Scale of Life
It has been estimated that there are approximately 35 trillion cells in an adult human being. All life on Earth evolved from a single cell over millions of years. Organisms can be unicellular (one cell) or multicellular (many cells — sometimes billions or trillions).
Section 3

The Life Processes

All living things carry out all of the following fundamental life processes. If an organism — or an entity — cannot carry out these processes, it is not considered living.

🌡️
Maintaining a Stable Internal Environment
Example: Humans sweating on a hot day to regulate body temperature (homeostasis).
↔️
Controlled Exchange of Materials
Example: Plants absorbing CO₂ and releasing O₂ through stomata during photosynthesis.
🐦
Response to Stimuli
Example: Birds migrating for winter in response to environmental cues.
⚡
Obtaining Energy and Chemical Elements
Example: Reptiles catching, killing and consuming prey for nutrients and energy.
🚛
Transport of Materials
Example: Blood carrying oxygen to muscle cells throughout the body.
🗑️
Removal of Waste
Example: Human kidneys filtering blood and producing urine to excrete waste products.
✂️
Cell Division
Example: A fertilised ovum dividing many times to form an embryo.
🦋
Growth and Development
Example: The metamorphosis of a caterpillar into a butterfly.
🐢
Independent Movement
Example: A turtle swimming through water.
🔄
Reproduction
Example: A bacterium dividing by binary fission to produce two new cells.
Section 4

Unicellular vs Multicellular Organisms

Organisms are classified by how many cells they consist of — from single-celled microorganisms to complex multicellular life forms.

🦠 Unicellular Organisms
Consist of one cell. Also called microorganisms because they generally cannot be seen with the naked eye.

Can be prokaryotes or eukaryotes. Found in almost all habitats including thermal vents, caves and deep underground.

Usually reproduce asexually and obtain nutrients through diffusion.
🌿 Multicellular Organisms
Consist of more than one cell — sometimes billions or trillions. All multicellular organisms are eukaryotes.

Examples include mushrooms, animals and plants. Consist of specialised cells with different structures and functions, produced through differentiation from stem cells.

Require organs and organ systems to coordinate life processes across cells.

Section 5

DNA & Hereditary Material

All cells contain hereditary material — DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid). Heredity is the passing of characteristics from one generation to the next. DNA enables cells to synthesise proteins and divide to produce new cells.

🧬
DNA Structure
Two complementary strands wound in a double helix. Each strand has a phosphate backbone and a sequence of four bases: Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Cytosine (C), Guanine (G).

🗜️
Chromosomes — Packaging DNA
Human cells contain approximately 2 metres of DNA — packed into chromosomes to fit inside a microscopic cell.

Chromosomes = DNA wrapped around histone proteins. This packaging condenses DNA and protects the genetic code from mutations.

Chromosomes are the structural unit of information in cells.
🔁
Why DNA is Complementary
The complementary base pairing (A-T, C-G) means each strand can be used as a template to rebuild the other strand. This is how DNA replication works — enabling genetic information to be passed from parent cells to daughter cells during cell division.
📍
Location of DNA
In eukaryotic cells: DNA is stored in the nucleus as linear chromosomes in pairs.

In prokaryotic cells: DNA is a single, circular chromosome located in the cytoplasm (nucleoid region) — there is no nucleus.

The differences are explored further in Chapter 1.2.
Section 6

The Cell Membrane

The cell membrane is found in all cells. It defines a cell by separating the intracellular (inside) environment from the extracellular (outside) environment.

📏
Structure
A very thin layer — only 0.01 μm thick. Consists of two layers of phospholipid molecules (a phospholipid bilayer) with a range of different proteins embedded within it.
🚪
Function
Controls the entry and exit of materials to and from the cell — acting as the boundary between the intracellular and extracellular environments. Facilitates communication between cells.
🔵
What It Defines
The cell membrane defines a cell. Everything inside it is intracellular; everything outside is extracellular. The membrane is the cell's boundary with its surroundings. Covered further in Chapter 1.5.
Section 7

Microscopes

Cells are microscopic and cannot be seen with the naked eye. Two major types of microscopes are used in biology — light and electron — each with different capabilities and applications.

🔦 Light Microscope
Light source
Uses visible light to illuminate specimens
Magnification
Generally ×400; maximum ~×2,000
Resolution
200 nm (lower resolution)
Specimens
Living or non-living (must be thin to allow light through)
Size & cost
Small, compact, portable. Affordable and low maintenance
Uses
Biology, medicine. Versatile, easy to use, real-time imaging of living samples
⚡ Electron Microscope
Light source
Uses a beam of electrons to illuminate specimens
Magnification
Generally ×500,000; maximum ~×2,000,000
Resolution
0.5 nm (extremely high resolution)
Specimens
Non-living (dead) specimens only
Size & cost
Big, bulky, requires permanent installation. Expensive and high maintenance
Types
SEM: 3D surface images · TEM: high-resolution internal cross sections

Components of a Light Microscope

Eyepiece Lens (×10)
The lens the observer looks through. Standard magnification of ×10.
Objective Lenses
Low (×4), medium (×10), and high power (×40). Combined with the eyepiece to give total magnification (e.g. ×10 eyepiece × ×40 objective = ×400 total).
Revolving Nosepiece
Rotates to bring different objective lenses into position over the specimen.
Stage
The platform where the microscope slide is placed for viewing.
Coarse Focus
Used to focus using low and medium power objective lenses. Makes large adjustments.
Fine Focus
Used to focus with the high-power objective lens. Makes small, precise adjustments.
Light Source
Illuminates the specimen from below. Light passes through the specimen and up to the lenses.
Condenser
Used to vary the intensity of light reaching the specimen — improving image quality.

📐 Worked Example
An image of an animal cell is 30 mm in size and has been magnified by a factor of ×3000. Determine the actual size of the cell.
1
Identify what you need to find: Actual size (A). Cover A in the IAM triangle → this leaves I ÷ M.
2
Substitute the known values: A = I ÷ M = 30 mm ÷ 3000
3
Calculate and convert units: A = 0.01 mm 0.01 mm = 10 μm ✓ Convert: 0.01 mm × 1000 = 10 μm (since 1 mm = 1000 μm)
📌 Important Reminders
Magnification has no units — write it as ×3000 not "3000 mm". Always check your units before calculating — image size is usually measured in mm from the slide/microscope field, and actual size is typically expressed in μm. Convert as needed using the chart above.
